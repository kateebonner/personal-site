Derivation

The figure on the home page is a free particle whose wavefunction starts as a Gaussian. This is where it comes from: a superposition of plane waves with a Gaussian spread of momenta, integrated in closed form. I first worked it out at Georgetown; this is the corrected version, with the two pieces that are easy to drop kept in, because they are what keep the wavefunction normalised as it spreads.

Setup

With no potential, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation is

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}, \qquad V = 0. $$

Each plane wave $e^{i(px - Et)/\hbar}$ with $E = p^2/2m$ solves it on its own, so any superposition of them does too. Take the amplitudes to be a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ in momentum, centred on $p_0$:

$$ \psi(x,t) = C \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} dp\; e^{-\frac{(p-p_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\; e^{\,i\left(\frac{px}{\hbar} - \frac{p^2 t}{2m\hbar}\right)}. $$

The integral

Everything in the exponent is at most quadratic in $p$, so the integral is a Gaussian one. Completing the square gives the identity

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-ap^2 + bp}\,dp = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\; e^{\frac{b^2}{4a}}, \qquad \operatorname{Re} a > 0. $$

Expanding $-(p-p_0)^2/2\sigma^2 = -p^2/2\sigma^2 + p\,p_0/\sigma^2 - p_0^2/2\sigma^2$ and collecting powers of $p$:

$$ a = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{i t}{2m\hbar}, \qquad b = \frac{p_0}{\sigma^2} + \frac{i x}{\hbar}, $$

with the leftover constant $-p_0^2/2\sigma^2$ riding along outside the integral. It helps to name the dimensionless time

$$ \tau = \frac{\sigma^2 t}{m\hbar}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad 4a = \frac{2}{\sigma^2}\,(1 + i\tau). $$

Then

$$ \frac{b^2}{4a} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2(1+i\tau)}\left[\frac{p_0^2}{\sigma^4} + \frac{2 i p_0 x}{\sigma^2\hbar} - \frac{x^2}{\hbar^2}\right] = \frac{p_0^2}{2\sigma^2(1+i\tau)} + \frac{i p_0 x}{\hbar(1+i\tau)} - \frac{\sigma^2 x^2}{2\hbar^2(1+i\tau)}. $$

The $\sigma^2$ cancels in the middle term. That term is the one that carries the motion, and $p_0 x/\hbar$ is dimensionless where $p_0 x/\sigma^2\hbar$ is not, which is a quick way to catch a slip there.

Result

Adding the constant $-p_0^2/2\sigma^2$ to the first term collapses the two $p_0^2$ pieces into one:

$$ \frac{p_0^2}{2\sigma^2}\left[\frac{1}{1+i\tau} - 1\right] = -\frac{p_0^2}{2\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{i\tau}{1+i\tau}. $$

So the wavefunction is

$$ \psi(x,t) = C\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{\sigma^2 x^2}{2\hbar^2(1+i\tau)} + \frac{i p_0 x}{\hbar(1+i\tau)} - \frac{p_0^2}{2\sigma^2}\,\frac{i\tau}{1+i\tau}\right]. $$

Fixing $C$ by $\int |\psi|^2\,dx = 1$ at $t = 0$ gives $C = (2\pi^{3/2}\sigma\hbar)^{-1/2}$, and since $\sqrt{\pi/a} = \sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2/(1+i\tau)}$, the prefactor tidies to

$$ \psi(x,t) = \left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\hbar}\right)^{\!1/2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+i\tau}}\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{\sigma^2 x^2}{2\hbar^2(1+i\tau)} + \frac{i p_0 x}{\hbar(1+i\tau)} - \frac{p_0^2}{2\sigma^2}\,\frac{i\tau}{1+i\tau}\right]. $$

What it says

Take the real part of the exponent, using $1/(1+i\tau) = (1 - i\tau)/(1+\tau^2)$, and complete the square in $x$. The $p_0^2$ term cancels exactly against the remainder of the square, leaving

$$ |\psi(x,t)|^2 = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\hbar\sqrt{1+\tau^2}}\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{\sigma^2\,(x - p_0 t/m)^2}{\hbar^2(1+\tau^2)}\right]. $$

Three things are in that line. The centre moves at the group velocity $p_0/m$. The width in position is

$$ \sigma_x(t) = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}\,\sqrt{1+\tau^2}, $$

which is the spreading of a free packet: a narrow spread in position is a wide spread in momentum, and the fast components pull away from the slow ones. And the peak falls as $(1+\tau^2)^{-1/4}$, exactly enough to keep the area under $|\psi|^2$ equal to one at every time.

The imaginary part of the exponent is the phase. Its $x$-dependence is no longer the plane wave $p_0 x/\hbar$ once $\tau \neq 0$: the term $\sigma^2 x^2\tau/2\hbar^2(1+\tau^2)$ curves it, so the local wavenumber rises ahead of the centre and falls behind it. In the figure that is the chirp: the helix winds tighter at the front of the packet and looser at the tail.

Two pieces that are easy to drop

Neither the prefactor $\sqrt{\pi/a}$ nor the $p_0^2$ term depends on $x$, so leaving them out changes nothing about the shape of the envelope at any one instant. What they control is the amplitude as a function of time. Without the prefactor the peak stays put while the width grows like $\sqrt{1+\tau^2}$, so the norm grows by the same factor; without the $p_0^2$ term the modulus picks up a spurious factor $e^{p_0^2\tau^2/\sigma^2(1+\tau^2)}$ on top. With both in place the norm is conserved, as it must be for a free particle.

On the home page

The figure uses $\hbar = m = 1$, $\sigma = 2$ (so the packet starts $\sigma_x(0) = 1/2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.35$ wide), $p_0 = 1$, and runs $t$ from $0$ to $5$ and back. Over that span the width grows twenty-fold while the centre moves five units, so the spreading is the whole event. The curve is $\psi$ drawn along $x$ with $\operatorname{Re}\psi$ up and $\operatorname{Im}\psi$ toward you, evaluated from the closed form above at every frame.
