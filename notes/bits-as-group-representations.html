Bits as group representations

Notes · 2026-09-14

A classical bit is a boolean; a quantum bit is a point on a sphere.

The cleanest way I know to say what separates them is to look at the groups that act on each. Each layer below is one group: its generators on the left, the states it can reach on the right, drawn from my notebook.

Classical information

The symmetry group of a classical bit is S2 = ⟨x | x2 = I⟩, a cyclic group of order 2. It acts on the set {0, 1}. The action is free and transitive, so the orbit of 0 is {0, 1} with trivial stabilizer. Choosing the outcomes as a basis of ℂ2 turns this action into a faithful representation ρ: S2 → U(2), which is how the classical group sits inside the quantum one.

Quantum information

The Pauli group is P1 = ⟨X, Y, Z⟩ ⊂ U(2), of order 16, with X2 = Y2 = Z2 = I. The six axis states 0, 1, +, −, +i, −i are the eigenstates of Z, X, and Y, and the vertices of an octahedron inscribed in the sphere. Under the Pauli group they form three orbits, the antipodal pairs {0, 1}, {+, −}, and {+i, −i}. Each pair is an eigenbasis of one generator, which fixes it, and the other two generators swap it.

The Clifford group

The Clifford group is C1 = ⟨H, S⟩ ⊂ U(2). It contains the Pauli group (S2 = Z, HZH = X) and is its normalizer: the unitaries that map Paulis to Paulis by conjugation. The three Pauli pairs merge into one orbit of six, with stabilizer {I, S, S2, S3}. It is the first layer to carry a classical state off the poles, and the last finite one.

A Lie group

SU(2) is a Lie group: a group that is also a smooth manifold. Its rotations Rx(θ), Ry(θ), Rz(θ) reach every point of the sphere. Here the group has dimension 3. Every group before it had dimension 0.

Six points, then a scatter that fills in, then the whole surface.
